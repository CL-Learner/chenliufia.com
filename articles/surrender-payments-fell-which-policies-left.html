Surrender Payments Fell. Which Policies Left?

Chen Liu, FIA · IFRS 17 reporting, part three

One line in my notes from an IFRS 17 results walkthrough still catches my eye. The chief actuary wanted a better explanation of an operating variance. I had reconciled the figures and prepared an estimate of my own. Looking at the same notes today, I think he was right to keep asking.

We were working towards implementation sign-off. I had the total liability and a comparison of projected and actual benefit counts. I took the percentage difference in the counts and applied it to the liability. That gave me a sense of the scale. I had used the same average liability per benefit for every product. A handful of departures from one product could matter more than several dozen from another. With the information I had assembled for the meeting, I could not tell which had happened.

In the first article I followed a result that looked wrong. In the second I followed a movement from the balance sheet into profit. There is a third question a reporting actuary faces even when the accounts reconcile. What has happened to the business, and what should we change in light of it?

A line worth stopping at

Consider an illustrative book of life policies closed to new business. Last year it paid 36 million to policyholders who surrendered. This year it paid 30 million. Finance has tied the payments to the ledger. Looking at cash alone, the company paid out six million less.

Now open the projection signed off before each year. Last year the actuaries expected 15 million of surrender payments. This year they expected 18 million, allowing for the policies still in force and their contractual surrender values. The excess over expectation has narrowed from 21 million to 12 million. Yet the book still paid two thirds more than this year’s allowance. After two years of paying well above expectation, I would want to know whether the surrender assumption still fits the policies on the books.

A program can put those four figures side by side. The reporting judgement is deciding why this line deserves the chief actuary’s time. On a closed book, every early exit changes the portfolio from which the company expects to earn its remaining profit. A fall in payments could coexist with a material change in the business left behind.

What would make me change the assumption?

Start with the simplest version of the 12 million gap. Suppose ten additional policies with surrender values of 1.2 million each left the book. A hundred additional policies worth 120,000 each would produce the same cash difference. There is a third possibility. The expected number left, but more of them held large policies. All three fit the management accounts. They call for different conversations with the person who signs the lapse assumptions.

I would ask policy administration for the surrender dates, products, policy years and amounts, with a distinction between full surrenders and partial withdrawals. I would agree the payments and timing to Finance’s total, then use the opening valuation assumptions and the exposure during the year to calculate expected exits in those same segments. That gives the chief actuary two comparisons to work with. How often did policies leave, and how much did each exit cost?

Suppose a few large policies explain most of the cash difference, while the surrender rate for that product is close to expectation. My first question would be how much future profit those particular policies carried. Elsewhere, a smaller product might contribute little to the cash variance yet show repeatedly high exit rates in the policy year when its surrender charge expires. That is where I would examine the assumption, looking at the experience on either side of the anniversary and how many policies were exposed. If the pattern held up, I would test a revised rate for that duration. The accounts alone cannot tell us why a customer chose to leave.

What did those departures do to earnings?

A shareholder might ask how much of the extra 12 million paid out reduced this year’s profit. I would first separate the investment component from the insurance claims. If the full 12 million difference came from current insurance claims, that claims variance alone would reduce this year’s profit by 12 million.¹

To see what those departures mean for future earnings, I would value the policies we expected to remain and the policies that actually remained, using the same assumptions at the same date and isolating the effect of surrenders. When a policy leaves early, the insurer avoids future claims and may save some maintenance expenses. It also gives up any premiums still to come. On a profitable policy, those premiums could have more than covered the remaining claims and expenses. That lost margin is easy to miss if all we look at is the surrender payment.

Suppose that comparison shows the group has lost four million of expected future net inflows. Its contractual service margin (CSM) falls by four million before release, leaving less profit to earn from future service. Which policies left matters for timing, too. Early exits can change the pattern of remaining coverage units and therefore how much CSM emerges as profit in each future year.²

What I would bring to the next walkthrough

I knew during implementation that the operating variance deserved more work. Getting the right records took time and cooperation from IT and Finance, and I had not arranged that work early enough. Suppose that calculation had reproduced 99% of the reported variance. I would still have needed to identify the policies behind the movement. If I were signing the assumptions, which surrender rate would I change, for which policies, and by how much?

In our quarterly reporting to the Bermuda Monetary Authority, we broke surrender experience down by product. One product accounted for most of the surrender variance on that basis. We were still using policy counts and average liabilities, but I could now ask IT for the surrender records of one product and explain why I wanted those first.

In a regular close I would start that conversation a quarter earlier. I would have Finance’s agreed surrender payments, policy administration’s exits and exposures, and valuation’s projection of the policies expected to remain, ready to compare. Then I could tell the chief actuary where experience warrants a review and tell management what it means for earnings on the remaining book. If the data supported only the first part of that explanation, I would say exactly how far we had got. That is the answer I wish I had been better prepared to give.
